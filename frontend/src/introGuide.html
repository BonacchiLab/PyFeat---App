Getting Started

This interface transforms videos into structured data about facial activity over time. No programming is required: follow the steps in the analysis workspace.

  1. Upload one or more videos in a supported format.
  2. Assign a Subject ID to each video.
  3. Choose the sampling mode that fits your research question.
  4. Optionally define the time windows for experimental stimuli.
  5. Select Extract data, wait for processing, and download each Parquet file.

The result is a dataset where each row represents a facial observation at a frame or within a one-second interval.

Preparing Your Videos

For more consistent detections, use videos where the face is visible for most of the recording and occupies a reasonable area of the image.

  • Avoid very dark lighting, strong backlighting, and abrupt exposure changes.
  • Avoid very small, partially occluded, or extremely rotated faces.
  • Check whether the video contains only the relevant participants, since multiple faces may be detected.
  • Keep quality and resolution similar across videos that will be compared.

Sampling Modes

  • Every frame: preserves the highest temporal resolution and is useful for fast phenomena, but creates larger datasets and takes longer.
  • 1 FPS - representative frame: selects approximately one frame per second. It is faster and more compact, but may miss brief movements.
  • Custom sampling: sets a target frequency in frames per second. The actual frequency is approximate and depends on the video's frame rate.

Choose the mode based on the time scale of your hypothesis. If a relevant expression is brief, a higher custom frequency may preserve it.

Experimental Metadata and Stimuli

Enable stimulus markers when you want to relate facial detections to specific moments in the experimental protocol.

  1. Indicate how many stimuli are in the session.
  2. Define the name of each stimulus.
  3. Enter the start and end seconds relative to the beginning of the video.
  4. Check that the intervals match the protocol; overlaps are preserved in separate columns.

The dataset includes one column, Stimulus_1, Stimulus_2, etc., for each stimulus. Each column contains its configured name inside that interval and is blank outside it. When intervals overlap, both stimulus columns are populated.

Facial Landmarks and Face Detection

Facial landmarks are reference points placed at specific facial locations, such as the eyebrows, eyes, nose, mouth, and facial outline. They describe facial geometry and movement over time.

Face detection locates the face in the image and may provide a bounding box, or face box. Landmarks and face boxes help verify whether the face was detected correctly and support the study of facial movement.

Action Units

An Action Unit (AU) describes an observable movement in a facial region according to the Facial Action Coding System (FACS). For example, an AU may represent eyebrow raising, cheek raising, or lip separation.

AUs describe facial movement; they are not emotions. An emotional expression can involve several AUs at once, and the same AU can occur in different contexts or expressions.

A higher AU value means that the model found stronger evidence for that facial movement. It should not be interpreted alone as proof of an emotion or intention.

AU Reference

The official Py-Feat reference connects each AU with its FACS name, involved musculature, commonly associated expressions, and supporting models.

  • AU1 - Inner Brow Raiser: movement of the inner part of the eyebrows.
  • AU4 - Brow Lowerer: drawing the eyebrows together and downward.
  • AU6 - Cheek Raiser: raising the cheeks and narrowing the eye opening.
  • AU12 - Lip Corner Puller: raising the corners of the mouth.
  • AU15 - Lip Corner Depressor: lowering the corners of the mouth.
  • AU25 - Lips Part: separating the lips without necessarily involving the jaw.

For the complete searchable table, see the official Py-Feat AU Reference.

Emotions and Facial Metrics

This application uses Py-Feat's default emotion model to estimate facial-expression categories such as Neutral, Happy, Sad, Surprise, Fear, Disgust, and Anger. These estimates are based on the facial appearance observed in each video frame.

Probabilities or values should be treated as indicators for analysis, not as direct measurements of a person's internal emotional state. Interpretation should consider context, individual variability, and the experimental design.

Understanding the Output Dataset

The Parquet file contains Py-Feat predictions and identifiers added by this interface.

  • subject_id: participant identifier assigned during upload.
  • input_file: source video name.
  • frame: frame number in the original video.
  • time_sec: approximate frame time in seconds in Every frame mode.
  • approx_time: readable time representation, such as 00:12.
  • second, time_start_sec, and time_end_sec: time interval created when data are aggregated by second.
  • Stimulus_1, Stimulus_2, etc.: one column per stimulus; a row can be marked in multiple columns when intervals overlap.
  • Action Units, emotions, landmarks, and other metrics: facial predictions produced by the Py-Feat model.

Always check the column names before analysis.

Interpreting the Data

1. Action Units (AUs): Separating Signal from Noise

  • Empirical thresholds: Treat AU readings below 0.10–0.15 as possible baseline noise. Calibrate this range against your detector, video quality, and participant baseline; do not apply it as a universal cutoff.
  • Anatomical co-occurrence: Look for anatomically plausible AU clusters. For example, AU6 with AU12 can support a smile interpretation. An isolated AU may reflect noise, a social gesture, or another movement.
  • Baseline deviation: Compare peaks with the same participant's resting, neutral face. A change from that baseline is often more informative than the raw value.
  • Temporal persistence: Filter isolated, single-frame spikes that may come from tracking artifacts. Look for movements lasting at least 3–5 consecutive frames (roughly 100–200 ms, depending on frame rate and sampling).

2. Emotion Probabilities: Value vs. Misinterpretation

  • Flat distributions: If several emotion scores fall between 0.20 and 0.30, treat the result as ambiguous rather than assigning a clear emotion. A single dominant score, such as above 0.70, is a stronger model signal, not proof of a person's psychological state.
  • Relative change: Focus on changes across conditions, such as a rise from 0.05 to 0.45, rather than treating an absolute probability as a definitive measurement.
  • Contextual synchrony: Align emotion scores with the exact stimulus timestamp. Compare changes before, during, and after the stimulus.

3. Advanced Tracking and Geometric Metrics (Critical Sanity Checks)

  • Face detection confidence (Face_Score): Flag or exclude data points below 0.90–0.95 for closer review. Choose a cutoff that fits your data and analysis goals.
  • Head pose (Pitch, Roll, Yaw): Treat rotations above roughly 15–20 degrees as a reason to inspect the frame. Larger rotations can distort visible facial geometry and affect AU estimates.
  • Landmark jitter and variance: Look for sudden, implausible jumps in landmark coordinates. These often indicate tracking failure, not a micro-expression.
  • Discontinuities and NaNs: Identify frames where the face is occluded or missing. Preserve these gaps as missing data; do not interpolate across them without a justified method.

Summary Checklist for Data Cleaning

Data Point Discard as Noise if... Keep as Valuable Signal if...
AU Values Readings stay below approximately 0.10–0.15, appear as isolated spikes, or lack a plausible AU cluster. Values rise above the participant's neutral baseline, form a plausible cluster, and persist across consecutive frames.
Emotion Values Scores are spread across several categories around 0.20–0.30, with no clear dominant category. One category becomes strongly dominant, or its score changes meaningfully across the stimulus context. Treat it as a model estimate, not a psychological fact.
Face_Score Confidence falls below your chosen review threshold, typically 0.90–0.95. Confidence meets your threshold and the face is visibly tracked well.
Head Pose Angles Pitch, Roll, or Yaw exceed roughly 15–20 degrees, or change abruptly. Pose remains stable enough for reliable facial visibility and comparison.
Landmark Coordinates Coordinates jump unnaturally, become discontinuous, or are missing during occlusion. Coordinates move smoothly and consistently with visible facial motion.

Good Practices and Limitations

  • Visually inspect a sample of the videos and results before statistical analysis.
  • Do not treat an emotion prediction as definitive evidence of an emotional state.
  • Record the sampling mode, stimulus intervals, and any data-exclusion decisions.
  • Take extra care with occlusion, glasses, masks, lighting, pose, low resolution, and multiple faces.
  • Consult the official documentation and model licenses before reusing results in other contexts.

Py-Feat recommends checking detections carefully. Computer-vision models can produce unexpected results in real-world videos, even when they perform well on benchmarks.
